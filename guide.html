How to use inmycalendar - and why a board works

How to use it, in ten seconds

  • Add a task - type into any of the three columns (To do, In progress, Done) and press Enter.
  • Move it along - drag it, or use the arrows on the card. The top of a column is your top priority.
  • Rename or reschedule - click a task to rename it, or use the calendar button on the card to move it to another day.
  • Switch the day - use the arrows by the date, or press T for today.
  • See the year - open the Calendar tab. Click any day to open it; colour a day to mark it as Leave, Travel, WFH or a Milestone.
  • Write a day note - open any day and add a line about what happened. It shows when you hover that day, and it comes out in the CSV export.
  • Add your public holidays - pick your country under Calendar setup and they appear on every year. National holidays show by default; tick the box to add regional ones too.
  • Add a countdown - put in a date that matters and it counts the days since, or until, it happens.

Everything saves automatically as you go. There is nothing to set up, and no account needed unless you want your board on more than one device.

Most task apps forget the calendar. Most calendars forget the tasks. inmycalendar keeps both in one place: a sticky-note board for what you're doing today, and a week-by-week view of the years around it.

The board

Three columns - to do, in progress, done - side by side on every screen size, phones included. Each task is a single line so a full day fits without scrolling. Type into a column to add straight to it. Drag to reorder, or use the arrows: the top of a column is your highest priority, so ranking costs nothing to express.

Switch the scope to week or month to see everything from that stretch of time in one read-only list; click any line to jump back to the day it belongs to. A column scrolls once it passes ten tasks, so a busy day never pushes the calendar off the screen.

The calendar

Years sit side by side, laid out by week rather than by month, so a long stretch of time reads at a glance. The arrows in the calendar's own header move the whole window a year at a time. Colour a day and the whole cell takes that colour.

Four things can mark a day and they never clash: the cell colour is your own category, a coloured line underneath is a public holiday (red for national, blue for regional), a ring is today, and a small dot means that day has tasks. Hovering a day shows the holiday name, your note, and the tasks on it.

How weeks are numbered

Everyone agrees a year has weeks. Nobody agrees which one is week 1. Four rules are in real use worldwide, so you pick the one your organisation follows, under Calendar setup.

  • First week with 4+ days in the new year - the majority rule. Week 1 is the first week with most of itself in the new year. With weeks starting Monday this is exactly ISO 8601, the international standard used across Europe and by most software.
  • Week containing the first Thursday (the default here) - ISO's pivot day, kept whatever day your week starts on. Several large retailers and their suppliers run their reporting weeks this way with a Sunday start, which gives a week 1 holding only three days of the new year. That is deliberate on their part, not a mistake.
  • First week fully inside the new year - week 1 never begins in December. Any days before it belong to the outgoing year's final week. Common in US federal payroll and in a number of broadcast and retail calendars.
  • Week containing 1 January - the simplest to explain, but it produces a 53rd week far more often and splits the year awkwardly.

Why Thursday? It is not a magic day. Thursday is the fourth day of a week that starts on Monday, so "the week containing the first Thursday" and "the first week with 4+ days" are two ways of saying the same thing - but only for a Monday start. Start your week on Sunday and the fourth day is Wednesday, and the two rules genuinely part company. That is why both are offered rather than one being quietly applied under the other's name.

Under the first three rules the last days of December often belong to the next year's week 1, or the first days of January to the previous year's final week. That is correct, not a mistake. With weeks starting Sunday, week 1 of 2026 begins on Sunday 28 December 2025 under the Thursday rule, and on Sunday 4 January 2026 under the majority rule.

You choose which day your week starts on separately, and all seven days are available - Monday for most of the world, Sunday across the Americas and much of Asia, Saturday across much of the Middle East. The two settings work together: the start day decides where each week begins, and the rule decides which one is first.

Keyboard

  • ← / → - previous or next day
  • T - jump to today  ·  N - new task
  • 1 / 2 / 3 - day, week, month  ·  B / C - board, calendar

Finding something later

Press / anywhere, or click the magnifier by the date, to search every task and day note you have written. Click a result to jump straight to that day.

Repeating a task

Hover a task and click the ↻ button to cycle it through every day, every week, every month and off. Repeats appear as ordinary tasks on the days they fall, so you can move, rename or delete any single one without touching the rest.

Day notes

Every day can carry a short note - what happened, a decision, anything worth remembering. Write it under the board, or open the day from the calendar. Done closes it, Cancel puts back what was there before, and Clear empties it. Notes show when you hover a day, and they come out in the CSV export.

Countdowns

Add any date that matters and it counts the days since, or until, it happens. Click a countdown's name to correct it, and switch between days, weeks, months and years with the dropdown beside it.

Where your data lives

By default, in this browser and nowhere else. No account is needed and nothing is uploaded.

Sign in (top right) if you want the same board on your laptop, phone and tablet. It is optional - it uses Google, Microsoft, GitHub or a one-time link sent to your email, and the app works exactly the same either way.

Either way you can take your data with you. Backup saves a JSON file, Restore reads one back, and Export tasks writes a CSV that includes your day notes and day colours. All three are in the footer.

What a Kanban board is

Kanban means “signboard” in Japanese. Toyota engineer Taiichi Ohno built the system in the late 1940s to control car production: cards moved across a board to signal what to build next, so nobody made parts that weren’t needed yet. Software teams borrowed it decades later, and it is now the default way most teams track work.

The idea is deliberately simple. Work moves left to right through columns, and you can see the whole flow at once:

  • To do - committed to, not started
  • In progress - actually being worked on right now
  • Done - finished

The one rule that matters: limit how much sits in “In progress”. Most people’s problem isn’t too few tasks - it’s five half-finished ones. Toyota’s insight was that starting less finishes more.

Using it with a team

The same three columns work for a team as for one person. A team typically agrees a cap on the middle column - say two items per person - and when it is full, nobody starts anything new until something is finished. Whatever has been sitting in “In progress” longest is usually the thing worth talking about at a stand-up.

A few things worth knowing

  • Toyota’s system was inspired by American supermarkets: shelves were only restocked once emptied, so nothing piled up in the back.
  • Kanban has no fixed sprints or deadlines built in - unlike Scrum, work simply flows through as capacity allows.
  • The measure teams care about most is cycle time: how long a task takes from starting to finishing. This app timestamps each column change, so an export gives you exactly that.
